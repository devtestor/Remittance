# Velo: Five-Day Great Lakes Payment Validation

## Decision to Make

Choose one customer segment, one payment route, and one recurring problem worth piloting. This sprint validates demand and delivery feasibility; it does not authorize a real-money launch.

Working mission: help people and businesses in the Great Lakes complete necessary payments at a lower total cost or with more reliable access and resolution.

Keep creator payouts, crypto, and new product features outside this sprint unless customer evidence makes them relevant. Preserve the existing app as a demonstration and testing foundation.

## Owners and Working Rules

- Founder: recruit participants, conduct interviews, contact potential partners, and make the decision.
- Research helper, if available: take consented notes and maintain the evidence sheet. The founder can do this alone.
- Technical lead: map existing capabilities to the selected workflow, run simulated tasks, and estimate missing work.
- Use five working days beginning when recruitment starts. Allow partner responses to remain pending beyond day five; pending evidence is not approval.
- Use remote interviews where appropriate. Do not travel through unsafe areas to meet a research target.
- No customer deposits, test transfers of real money, passwords, OTPs, identity documents, or unredacted account statements.
- Participation is voluntary. Ask permission before recording; written notes are sufficient. Redact names, phone numbers, account numbers, and transaction references from receipts.
- Use participant codes in the research sheet. Keep contact details separately, restrict access, and agree a retention period with participants.
- Findings describe the people interviewed, not the entire regional market.

## Recruitment Target

Aim for 20 customer interviews and three provider or operations conversations:

| Group | Target | What to investigate |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Rwanda-based cross-border traders | 8 | Supplier payments, FX, repeat costs, delays, account restrictions |
| Eastern DRC traders or recipients reachable safely | 8 | Actual availability of receiving, spending, and cash-out routes by locality |
| Family senders or recipients on those routes | 4 | Small transfers, recipient costs, travel and waiting |
| Providers, agents, or humanitarian payment operators | 3 | Delivery coverage, liquidity, pricing, limits, accepted activity |

Distinguish ordinary merchant trade from cryptocurrency P2P trading in the notes. Do not assume their provider requirements or failure causes are interchangeable.

Recruit through existing relationships, trader associations, and referrals. Avoid a sample made only of friends who want to encourage the project. If recruitment falls short, record the shortfall and narrow the conclusion.

Outreach message:

> I am researching the difficulties people face when sending, receiving, or paying suppliers across the Great Lakes. Could we discuss your most recent payment for 20 minutes? This is research, not a sales pitch. I will not ask for your money, password, OTP, or identity documents. You can skip any question.

## Day 1: Understand Actual Transactions

Morning:

1. Create the evidence sheet using the columns below.
2. Contact roughly 30 potential participants to schedule 20 interviews over three days.
3. Start with the communities and routes you can reach directly; do not preselect US/UK-to-Rwanda.

Afternoon:

1. Complete six interviews: three Rwanda traders, two eastern DRC participants, and one family sender or recipient.
2. Reconstruct each person's last transaction from funding to usable money.
3. Ask for a redacted receipt only if they are comfortable sharing one.

End-of-day deliverable: six transaction stories and a list of recurring obstacles. Separate participant reports from verified documents and your assumptions.

## Day 2: Quantify the Pain

1. Complete eight more interviews: three Rwanda traders, three eastern DRC participants, and two family senders or recipients.
2. Identify the three most frequent problems within the sample.
3. Collect current prices from the routes participants actually use, including destination withdrawal charges.
4. Send specific questions to at least three potential providers or payment operators; request written answers and pricing.
5. Record whether a restriction is a branch closure, lack of cash, account freeze, unavailable transfer route, identity problem, or provider refusal. These require different solutions.

End-of-day deliverable: 14 interviews, an initial cost comparison, and three candidate problems.

## Day 3: Select One Candidate

1. Finish six interviews: two Rwanda traders, three eastern DRC participants, and one family sender or recipient.
2. Review evidence for each candidate using the scorecard below.
3. Follow up with provider contacts. Record explicit coverage and exclusions; do not treat an unanswered email or a marketing page as operational confirmation.
4. Select one segment, source location, destination locality, currency pair, payment purpose, and amount range.
5. Write a one-sentence hypothesis and identify the assumption most likely to invalidate it.

Hypothesis template:

> For [specific users] making [specific recurring payment] from [origin] to [destination], Velo can improve [measurable outcome] through [confirmed or proposed route], while earning a sustainable margin.

End-of-day deliverable: one candidate hypothesis, an alternatives comparison, and a list of unresolved dependencies. If no credible candidate emerges, use days four and five for more discovery rather than forcing a prototype test.

## Day 4: Test a Simulated Service

1. Prepare one simple flow using the existing app or a clickable prototype: enter recipient, see total cost and timing, confirm, track, and request help.
2. Clearly label all amounts, statuses, timing, and partner coverage as simulated where they are not verified.
3. Run five individual sessions with people from the selected segment. Participants can be returning interviewees.
4. Ask participants to complete tasks without coaching. Record where they hesitate or misunderstand.
5. Show a delayed-payment example and a refund-pending example. Check whether they understand that a pending refund is not returned money.
6. Present an evidence-backed price or an explicitly hypothetical price. Ask which current method they would replace and why.
7. Ask whether they will join a later pilot and bring their next eligible transaction once the service is approved. This is a signal of interest, not revenue or proof of retention.

End-of-day deliverable: five usability records, pilot interest, objections, and a preliminary unit-economics model. Do not spend this day polishing branding.

## Day 5: Decide and Set the Next Milestone

Morning:

1. Recheck price comparisons and distinguish introductory promotions from repeat pricing.
2. Review provider answers, operational exclusions, and unresolved legal questions with an appropriately qualified adviser when available.
3. Test whether the cost model still works at lower volume, with delays, and with higher support costs.
4. Classify the result using the decision gates below.

Afternoon:

1. Produce the one-page decision memo.
2. Contact consenting pilot candidates with an accurate update; do not promise availability or delivery dates that are unconfirmed.
3. Create a two-week backlog limited to the selected workflow and its prerequisites.

End-of-day deliverable: proceed, investigate further, or stop this candidate, with evidence and named owners for next actions.

## Interview Script: 20 Minutes

Begin by explaining the research purpose and obtaining consent for notes.

1. Tell me about the last time you sent, received, or paid someone across the border.
2. Where did it start and where did the recipient need to use the money?
3. What was the payment for? How often do you make a similar payment?
4. Walk me through every step, including any agent, intermediary, trip, or withdrawal.
5. How much did you pay in total? How much did the recipient receive and actually use?
6. What currency did you start with and finish with? Was the rate shown before payment?
7. How long did each step take? What delivery time were you told?
8. What went wrong, if anything? What reason or evidence did the provider give?
9. Who helped resolve it? What did resolution cost in time or money?
10. Which other method did you try or consider, and why did you reject it?
11. If only one part could improve, which would matter most?
12. May we contact you for a simulated test? Do you know another person with a different experience?

For frozen funds: record duration, stated reason, documents requested, appeal channel, and outcome. Do not infer misconduct or arbitrary treatment from a single account of events.

For limited banking access: ask what works today, by locality, including merchant spending and mobile money. Do not infer that all eastern DRC locations have the same access conditions.

## Evidence Sheet

Use one row per actual transaction, allowing multiple rows per participant:

| Field | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Participant code and segment | No public personal identifiers |
| Interview date and consent | Notes or recording permission |
| Source and destination | Country and relevant locality |
| Purpose and frequency | Merchant trade, family support, crypto P2P, other |
| Date and route | Actual provider and intermediaries used |
| Funding currency and total spent | Include separately charged fees |
| Received currency and amount | Before withdrawal, if known |
| Amount usable after withdrawal | Distinguish wallet balance from cash |
| Additional expenses | Travel, agent, intermediary charges |
| Expected and actual timing | State units; record unknowns |
| Failure or restriction | Participant's report and provider's explanation separately |
| Evidence quality | Redacted receipt, participant recollection, provider quote |
| Existing alternative | Cost, availability, reason not used |
| Biggest requested improvement | Participant's own words |
| Follow-up interest | Consent to contact, no deposit |

Do not fill missing fees or timings with guesses.

## Cost and Business Model

Compare routes at the same time, amount, currencies, funding method, destination method, and delivery expectation. A wallet credit and cash in hand are different outcomes.

For a like-for-like comparison, fix the sender's total budget and compare the recipient's usable amount after all route and withdrawal charges. Record travel expense and time separately if not already deducted.

If a reliable reference FX rate is available:

`effective cost % = 100 * (1 - usable destination amount / (sender total spent * reference FX rate))`

The reference rate must be expressed as destination currency per source unit and timestamped. It is a comparison benchmark, not a rate Velo is guaranteed to obtain. Do not add an FX spread twice when it is already reflected in the usable amount.

Velo's contribution per transfer:

`fee revenue + disclosed FX revenue - collection - settlement - payout - FX execution/liquidity cost - expected fraud/returns - variable compliance/support cost`

Document which costs are included in partner quotes to avoid double counting. Separately estimate fixed operating costs, customer acquisition, reserve requirements, and working capital. Positive contribution alone does not mean the company is profitable.

## Provider Questions

- Do you currently support this exact origin, destination locality, currency, and payment purpose?
- Which entity holds funds and performs each regulated activity? What approvals would Velo need for the proposed role?
- Do you accept merchant payments and, separately, cryptocurrency P2P-related activity?
- What are the total charges, minimum commitments, FX terms, limits, reserves, and settlement times?
- Can recipients spend the funds digitally? Where can they withdraw, at what cost, and with what cash availability?
- What happens during outages, insufficient agent liquidity, rejected payments, and account holds?
- What proves payment or refund completion? What are the appeal and escalation procedures?
- What reporting, customer checks, data handling, and security requirements apply?

Request written confirmation. A foreign license, sandbox admission, or technology-vendor description should not be assumed to cover the full proposed service.

## Candidate Scorecard

Score each candidate from 1 to 5 and explain each score with evidence:

| Dimension | Weight |
| --- | ---: |
| Severity of the demonstrated problem | 25% |
| Frequency of the transaction | 20% |
| Feasibility of a supported delivery route | 25% |
| Sustainable economics | 20% |
| Ability to recruit and support customers | 10% |

The score helps compare opportunities. It cannot override an unavailable route or unacceptable risks to participants.

## Decision Gates

These are proposed internal thresholds, not market statistics. Set them before reviewing results.

Proceed to a controlled pilot preparation only if:

- At least eight interviews concern the chosen segment, and at least five independently describe the same recurring problem.
- At least five actual transaction examples support the baseline; document how many have receipt evidence.
- At least three users from the segment explicitly agree to a later pilot without a deposit.
- At least four of five simulated-test participants understand the recipient amount, timing, and pending/failure state without coaching.
- The proposed route has credible provider coverage and pricing, with a written path to required approvals.
- The model suggests either at least 20% lower total cost for matched transactions or a specifically measurable reliability/access improvement valued by users. Both remain hypotheses until a live pilot measures them.
- Economics can support positive contribution without indefinite promotional subsidies.

Investigate further when customer pain is clear but pricing, coverage, approval, or liquidity evidence remains missing. Set a deadline and owner for each unanswered question; do not describe this as pilot readiness.

Stop or change the candidate when customers rarely experience the problem, an existing option already solves it adequately, costs leave no sustainable margin, or the service cannot be delivered through an acceptable operating arrangement.

## One-Page Decision Memo

1. Selected user and exact payment route.
2. Problem, frequency, and supporting transaction examples.
3. Existing alternatives and why customers would switch.
4. Proposed service and measurable improvement.
5. Provider evidence, cost model, and missing approvals.
6. Simulated-test findings and pilot interest.
7. Decision, unresolved assumptions, owners, and next review date.

Before any real-money pilot, review Velo's financial correctness: atomic state/ledger updates, duplicate/concurrent events, custody arrangements, and confirmed refund settlement. A payout failure alone must not be represented as money already returned to the customer.
